I recently finished reading Ian Bogost's book [*Alien Phenomenology, or What It's Like to Be a Thing*](https://www.upress.umn.edu/9780816678983/alien-phenomenology-or-what-its-like-to-be-a-thing/). This isn't my usual type of leisure reading, but over the past few months I've wanted to read more HCI-relevant philosophy. My favorite points in my PhD came when I felt challenged to consider alternative perspectives to my own. I chose to read this book in the hopes of creating this type of moment and to finally follow the years-old suggestion of a colleague of mine.

Back when I was a baby graduate student fresh out of an engineering undergrad, I was working on sonifications as part of [IMAGE](https://image.a11y.mcgill.ca) with my far more artistic and philosophically informed coworker, Florian Grond. He gave me many readings to get up to speed on the technical and design sides of sonifications, but also recommended that reading up on object-oriented ontology might be beneficial. With the dumb confidence of someone who had never taken a philosophy class, I said I would check it out, found a reasonably cited paper on it, and proceeded to have no idea what the authors were talking about. I completely lacked the knowledge to understand the references and arguments the authors made and was entirely thrown off by the style of philosophical writing[^1] as someone who had only ever previously read and wrote engineering and music technology papers.

As I began using more qualitative methods in my research and began to understand [how poorly](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3491101.3516392) [we in HCI](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3544548.3581203) [tend to use them](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3772318.3790417),[^2] I went down something of a rabbit hole trying to understand what it meant to "do science well" and slowly became less of a stranger to philosphy. Several months ago, I came back across Bogost's *Alien Phenomenology* and realized it seemed like the perfect introduction: 165 pages of practical object-oriented ontology. So I dediced to *finally* follow through on my promise to Florian and give the book a read.

## Alien Phenomenology

*Alien Phenomenology* consists of five chapters each dedicated to a particular concept. In the first, eponymous chapter, the basics of object-oriented ontology (or "OOO" for brevity) are introduced. The core of OOO is that all things, living or not, and the relations between them should be subjects of philosophical interest. Rather than focus on the interconnections between these different objects at the expense of establishing clear boundaries between them, Bogost focuses his analysis on objects as "units" that are part of and encompass complex systems. "Counterintuitively, a system and a unit represent three things at once: for one, a unit is isolated and unique. For another, a unit encloses a system&mdash;an entire universe's worth. For yet another, a unit becomes part of another system&mdash;often many other systems&mdash;it jostles around" (p. 25). With this basis established, Bogost explains that "alien phenomenology" is a process of speculation about what it means for these non-human units to be what they are. With a scope for the book set, the remaining chapters are dedicated to the practice of alien phenomenology.

"Ontography" discusses the cataloguing of these units and their relationships, particularly deliving into lists and the power of the "Latour litany." Lists provide an open-ended structure that I quite like, manging to be evocative of the many potential connections between their contents while avoiding the potential pitfalls of an explicit explanation of how they fit together. The absence of organization allows any element to serve as a focal point to the reader, something that may be far harder in a more structured explanation. Other techniques, such as exploded view diagrams, photographs, and games, are discussed as means of collecting and showing what exists as well.

In "Metaphorism," the focus changes from the system to unit as Bogost begins to speculate about the subjective experiences of non-humans, both living and not. The title of the chapter comes from the argument that we humans must rely on metaphor as a means of doing alien phenomenology. As Bogost writes, "&hellip;to understand how something operates on its surroundings, or they on it, is not the same as understanding how that other thing *understands* those operations" (p. 63). We can only speculate as to the subjective experience of objects, but attempting to understand and grapple with that experience can be meaningful. The main example given in the book is that of two camera sensors that operate via different mechanisms. In one, increasing light sensitivity leads to the appearance of noise on the image. In the other, this same operation leads the colors in the image to change. Their mechanisms are compared and contrasted with human vision as two alien forms of vision rather than imperfect attempts to replicate what humans "objectively" see.

The subtitle of the next chapter, "Carpentry," is "Constructing Artifacts that Do Philosophy." Bogost urges the reader to view philosophy as a practice and use his alien phenomenology in what we create rather than just what already exists around us. As an example, he describes his program *I am TIA* which approximates the perspective of the TIA video chip of the Atari Video Computer System. It provides a metaphor to understand the TIA's operation beyond familiarizing oneself with its technical documentation or the accounts of people who have worked with it. After once more encouraging the reader to not treat philosophy as a written or passive endeavor, Bogost ends the book with "Wonder." Using television as a framing, he identifies how attempts to center non-humans frequently serve to benefit humans. For example, Alton Brown's in-depth explanations of the physical processes that occur in cooking on the show *Good Eats*[^3] are ultimately present to help the viewer create a better product for consumption. Bogost encourages the reader to pursue wonder or awe through alien phenomenology and OOO more generally as a way to avoid the oversimplifications that can occur if practicality is the primary concern.

As someone with a background in engineering, I quite appreciated the pragmatism of the book. It is not pragmatic in the sense that it provides a clear procedure one can follow to attain a material benefit, but in its orientation towards philosohy as an activity. The reader is passively and directly encouraged to chart the units of the systems around them, grapple with the experiences of these units through metaphors, and through this construct artifacts like *I am TIA* that facilitate these practices or otherwise "do philosophy." I am admittedly a bit unsure where to draw the boundary between Bogost's carpentry and typical HCI practices, and how one can do alien phenomenology in a way that is compatible with inherently anthropomorphic engineering and design projects. Ultimately, I began writing this blog as a way to begin working through these questions, even though I am well aware that any answers I can form will be incomplete without practice.

In that spirit, I think it is best to start with an example. During my PhD, I spent countless hours creating audio-haptic effects and tools for creating audio-haptic effects. My focus rested on the experience of people encountering and trying to use these effects and tools, but at no point did I sit down and consider what it may be like to be an element of these tools or an effect in and of itself. Looking now at the [audio-haptic version](https://github.com/JRegimbal/haptic-swatch) of the [tool presented in my Eurohaptics paper](https://rdcu.be/dZbCt), let's try some philosophy.

## Audio-Haptic Design

The tool I designed consists of several units. The decision of where to draw lines between these units is to a large degree arbitrary, so I will use the boundaries present in the documentation I wrote as a guide. Following from here, the system of the tool connects: a designer, a laptop, a Haply 2DIY, headphones, reinforcement learning agents, buffers of recent agent actions, a virtual plane for effects, a variable number of audio-haptic effects, buffers of recent effects, a button to create effects, keys to delete effects, sliders to modify the parameters of effects, buttons to send positive or negative signals to an agent, a manual-agent control toggle, sliders an a Processing physics simulation, a Python OSC server, a SuperCollider audio server.

The story I typically tell about the tool is roughly as follows: the tool consists of a workspace where the designer can add circular audio-haptic elements. Each of these elements has different properties (e.g., texture, pitch) that can be controlled via dials or sliders and can be experienced by moving a virtual representation of the 2DIY within the bounds of the element. If the end effector of the 2DIY is simultaneously within two the bounds of two elements, the audio and haptic effects of them will be displayed simultaneously.

Each element also has an associated agent that, when enabled, will slightly adjust audio and haptic parameters at a constant rate. The choice of what to adjust and how will at first be made randomly, but the designer can give positive or negative feedback to the agent via key or button presses. This is used to train the agent by crediting a positive or negative award to recent pairs of states and actions. As such, the agent should, over time, take actions that are more interesting to the designer. To accelerate this process, the designer may manually specify which parameters or ranges of parameters the agent is permitted to act within, decreasing the size of the action and state spaces considerably.

What is it to be one of these agents? While a user would perceive them as being associated with the many audio-haptic elements in the workspace, each agent is completely disconnected from one another. Its entire world is a time sequence of parameter values and changes in them free of context. These values are put in a queue for a short period of several seconds before vanishing from existence. During that time, the agent may receive a reward signal which it will distribute between the state-action pairs in the queue. These new values will then be stored in a second list from which the agent will randomly sample values for training. Upon receiving a reward or periodically, the agent will perform a training process to update a function to determine which action is most likely to result in positive reward given some set of parameter values. The agent applies this function periodically subject to a number of constraints on which actions it may select.

While it may not be the most interesting subject of analysis, the agent in this system is certainly alien. It is meant to encourage one's creativity while not remotely perceiving what is being designed in the same manner or at the same scale as a human. To it, the designer is nothing but a reward-producing process and manipulator of parameter values, although from the agent's perspective there would not be anything connecting these two roles. With this in mind, it is easy to see why in several participants in my studies became frustrated by the agent not respecting their focus on either audio or haptic properties, or helping to create several coordinated effects. People expected an agent that collaborates with them to work like them. Future work on this project could take one of two directions: creating an agent whose perception of audio-haptic design is more similar to humans or, perhaps more fitting with Bogost's concept of carpentry, construct a tool that helps the designer understand the agent's perspective of the task through the use of different interactive features.

## HCI Practice

In this way, I think this "carpentry" provides an interesting parallel to Norman's description of the system model and mental model in *The Psychology of Everyday Things*. Obviously one is far more human-centered than the other, but if we focus on the alien technologies that fill our world then a great challenge for HCI practitioners is avoiding situations where people operate a system "incorrectly" due to a flawed, often oversimplified model of its operation. Alien phenomenology and OOO generally seem to provide a route towards understanding  system models in an alternative way to source code, circuit diagrams, or other schematics.[^4] Carpentry through projects like *I am TIA* further translate this perspective into metaphors for us humans, a potentially powerful resource when working with complex computer systems.

As someone newly working in industry, I am forced to ask myself if it is possible to primarily pursue "wonder" when doing alien phenomenology in that context. Physical and non-physical elements of the power system seem to me like very interesting units to apply this practice to, but a request to do any project with the explicit aim of building a sense of wonder rather than deliver a benefit to customers is a complete non-starter. What's more, I am not convinced that one *can* do alien phenomenology on the grid without an element of human benefit being present. It is quite literally a necessary part of me writing this blog post and for you to read it. An "obsolete" technology like the Atari Video Computer System is presently purposeless from the perspective of humans. We can attempt to find reasons to use it in the modern day, Bogost gives skill transfer to other embedded programming as an example, but we must search for these reasons. No searching is necessary for the power grid, the internet, cellular networks, or any of their components.

Perhaps it is worth distinguishing between HCI practitioners and other stakeholders in this context. For the HCI practitioner, I fully understand the role of wonder as a motivator to grapple with the complexity of the alien and construct metaphors that do it justice. Settling for an explanation that is "good enough" is the source of many usability problems in our world. For managers, customers, and even the practitioner though, I think that anthropocentric concerns are unavoidable and even necessary. I entered this line of work for the same reason as many others: I wanted to help solve problems that others face. Having this sense of meaning to one's practice of alien phenomenology seems perfectly acceptable to me as long as it is coupled with wonder. Personally, I hope to use both to fuel my own application of these concepts in my future projects.
