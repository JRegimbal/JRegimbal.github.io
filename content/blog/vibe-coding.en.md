Recently, I was encouraged to try vibe coding and LLMs in my work. I never felt much of a desire to use them in the past, having disengaged from the initial wave of enthusiasm around LLMs due to concerns about how these models were being produced and their effects on users and society. Since then, I have mainly used them in one-off tests. I also just am not particularly enamoured with chat interfaces and feel that I have enough of a DIY orientation that even if LLMs were "perfect" they would be my equivalent of a box of [cake mix that doesn't require an egg](https://www.bbc.com/future/article/20171027-the-magic-cakes-that-come-from-a-packet). That is, I just think there's value in doing some work yourself.

That all said, I have been curious what it is like to actually use these technologies, especially in software where it seems LLM adoption is now the norm. Over the past few months, I gave LLMs and "vibe coding" an honest try. This post will focus on my experience of using LLMs in various tasks, primarily front-end programming, as an HCI researcher with formal training in software engineering who has been doing software development in some form for more than half her life.

First, the design tools. I briefly used Figma Make in some wireframing work, and was broadly disappointed. The software was quite good at generating polished UIs that comply with contemporary web standards and norms (at least upon a cursory inspection), but it was not easy to iterate using pre-existing wireframes. It felt as if my entire work process needed to be abandoned if I wanted to use this software. This was a hard ask considering that Figma Make did not really produce designs that responded to the problem at hand. Trying to refine its wireframes through prompting just elicited a succession of interfaces my coworker compared to menus to build a cell phone plan. Since I do not work for a telecom, it ultimately was better to just proceed without the tool.

Second, vibe coding. As someone who still primarily codes (and writes this blog post) in a minimal vim editor, I was pleasantly surprised by GitHub Copilot's ability to generate relevant templates and implement basic interactive features. It overrode my preferred styling, which was quite annoying, but at first the LLM's suggestions seemed to work quite well.

Now for the inevitable "but": after a little while, I started to see *extremely* wrong suggestions appear in both the "agentic" chat interface and through line completions. At first, I was merely annoyed by some modifications being overly complicated for the problem at hand or not fitting the emerging structure of the program. These issues could be manually corrected easily, but they interrupted me and made it difficult to enter a flow state. Then the LLM started to propose solutions that plainly did not work or did work but added layers of technical debt that I would need to sort out later.

Here are some quick examples: in one instance, a coworker asked for help implementing a certain feature after attempting to make a solution with an LLM, adding hundreds of lines of attempted fixes in the progress. After a thirty minute chat to understand what their underlying problem was, my fix was five lines. In another instance, I wanted to inspect the data served by a web server. The LLM "hallucinated" a procedure to download the data that was completely divorced from reality, but plausible to how a server *could* work. Interestingly, as I tested its responses and saw them fail, the LLM would generate these follow-up answers that were both sycophantic and utterly patronizing. For example, it would praise me for being detail oriented enough to actually test its response, but then say something along the lines of "well obviously this solution would never work in practice for *n* technical reasons that anyone would understand." I ultimately just audited the network traffic in the browser and analyzed the payload I needed using some of my favorite tools: jq and curl. In another instance, the LLM took an instruction to implement a straightforward feature, misidentified a recent addition to Javascript I used in my code as not actually existing, and began to rewrite the work I had done while also completely changing the pattern I wanted the LLM to extend. I just did the work myself after that.

In all of these cases, the thorough-line I saw was that LLMs were perfectly capable of making decent results in simple, scoped contexts, but they break down when the logic of the software or use case becomes too complex. Using them also added technical debt that I later needed to fix myself, which was far higher due to my relatively underdeveloped mental model of how the software operated at a lower level. I had recently read Starr and Storey's recent [paper on software troubleshooting](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.10540v2), and generally just found it resonating with much of my experience in this very informal experiment. What I found most interesting is that as I started using LLMs more, I felt additional self-imposed pressure to deliver tangible results faster despite my awareness that much of the time I was "saving" was merely delayed to the near future as debugging/refactoring/maintenance. I am lucky enough to have the time to do such work, but I know that is not universal for software development in 2026. At the end of this experience, I am left with the sense that we need to take a step back and consider the costs of prioritizing the creation of new features and code above all else. In particular, where do we consider reliability and maintainability in the life of software?
